# The gap map

Four questions, answered from full text only. This is what makes the brief worth more
than a literature summary.

## Wants — what the lab is trying to establish next

Look in:
- The **final two paragraphs of the Discussion**. This is where a PI writes their next
  grant aim in disguise.
- **Explicit future-direction sentences.** Phrases like *"a key future direction"*,
  *"Future work will…"*, *"remains to be determined"* are not hedges — they are a
  statement of intent. Quote them verbatim in the brief.
- **Talk abstracts and closing slides.** A PI's closing remarks in a recent seminar
  ("currently we're looking for…") are the most current statement of direction that
  exists in public.
- **Translational asides.** A sentence about a therapeutic avenue in a basic-science
  paper is usually load-bearing for the lab's funding.

## Cannot — what they currently can't do

The richest and least-read category. Tells:

- **A workaround in Methods.** If they built a clonal line, a new allele, or a second
  model to rescue a failing experiment, the original approach failed. Find out why.
- **Self-stated underestimation.** *"our results probably underestimate…"* means an
  experiment did not fully work and they said so.
- **Reagent or model constraints.** A marker chosen for specificity over convenience; a
  surface marker that doesn't exist; a state that cannot be generated in vitro.
- **An assay conspicuously absent.** If a paper establishes a cell state but never
  cultures it, ask why. Often the answer is that they cannot.
- **The acknowledged-limitation paragraph**, if the journal requires one.

## Unproven — claimed but not shown

- **"consistent with"** = not demonstrated here. It is the most common load-bearing hedge
  in high-profile papers. Every instance is a potential question.
- **"suggests" / "implies" / "may"** attached to a mechanistic claim.
- **One system generalised to another.** A result proven in one organ, cell type, or
  species and then described in the abstract, title, or a talk as general. Check whether
  the generalisation is supported anywhere in the data. This asymmetry is often the single
  strongest question available.
- **Citation-backed claims** where the cited work is in a different system.

## Fit — where the candidate contributes

The constrained step. Build the intersection of **Cannot/Unproven** with the candidate's
**demonstrated competence** — the things they have actually done and can defend under
questioning.

Competence is not the same as tools. "I built X" is a tool. "I can make a construct work
in a system where the standard approach fails, and I know why it fails" is a competence.
The second transfers; the first does not, and pitching the first violates the skill's
primary rule.

Write the Fit row as a **question the candidate can ask**, never as an offer. Compare:

- Bad: *"My system could be used to generate your cell state in vitro."*
- Good: *"You note that reductionist induction of this state is a goal. Is the barrier
  the inducing signal, or the readout — do you have a way to tell you've got the real
  state rather than something that merely scores on the signature?"*

The second shows the candidate understands the problem's actual shape. If their tool is
relevant, the PI asks about it next, unprompted.

## Output shape

A table the candidate can read in 90 seconds:

| Wants | Cannot | Unproven | My angle |
|---|---|---|---|

Keep it to the 4–6 rows that matter. A twenty-row gap map is a sign you have not decided
what is important, and it is useless at 6am.
