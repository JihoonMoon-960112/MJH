# Reading the candidate

Do this first, from the actual CV. Errors here surface in documents the PI reads side by
side with the CV, and they are fatal to credibility.

## Authorship — get it exact

Three distinct categories, never collapsed:

- **Sole first author** — fully theirs to narrate.
- **Co-first author** (usually `*`) — theirs to narrate, with the contribution specified.
  Note where in the co-first list they sit; it is sometimes meaningful.
- **Middle author** — a contribution, not a project. Describing a middle-authorship as if
  it were one's own project is the kind of error a PI notices immediately and does not
  forget.

Also record **publication status accurately**: published, in press, under review,
preprint. Do not upgrade anything. Build a table and have the user check it before it goes
into any artifact.

## Competence vs. tools

Separate these explicitly, because only one of them is portable:

- **Tool** — the specific system, construct, or assay they built.
- **Competence** — what they can do that transfers. Debugging a method that fails for
  non-obvious reasons; building something that works across species; designing a clean
  genetic control; knowing why an approach breaks.

The Fit row of the gap map draws on competence only. Tools stay in the one-line
background statement.

## The background statement

Four sentences, 35–45 seconds spoken. Longer and it becomes a talk.

1. **The bottleneck** — the field-level problem, in one sentence. Start here, never with
   the history of the technique; the PI has used it for twenty years.
2. **Why existing solutions fall short** — concrete, specific.
3. **What we changed** — the unit, the principle. One sentence.
4. **What was mine** — the candidate's own contribution.

Leave 3 and 4 as `[in my own words]` placeholders for the candidate to fill if the
mechanism details or contribution split are not verifiable from the CV and papers you
have. **Do not guess them.** A fabricated mechanism detail collapses on the first
follow-up question.

Attach the **limitation in the same breath**. Having stated what existing approaches fail
at, the candidate will be asked where their own approach breaks. A scientist who names
their own failure mode first, plainly and without defensiveness, gains more credibility in
that one sentence than in the rest of the call. Prepare it deliberately; it is the same
content as the hardest expected question, so write it once and use it in both places.

## Blanks that stay blank

Mark and leave:

- The specific failure mode of their own work
- Why this lab, personally
- Any account of a difficult advisor relationship
- Career motivation

These must be in the candidate's voice or they do not survive contact.
